speakin

A virtual reality app for practicing spoken English inside scenes that feel real. I spent a year researching, designing, filming and testing it for my design degree at the University of São Paulo.

Leticia Almeida · advised by Prof. Dr. André Leme Fleury

Watch the prototype videos on YouTube ↗

People study English for years and still can't hold a conversation

In 2013 only 5.1% of Brazilians over 16 said they had any knowledge of English (British Council). The ones who pay for a language school often leave with grammar and no confidence to speak.

I wanted to know why, from the learners' side, before deciding what to build.

What I did before designing anything

2
language schools, sitting in on classes
6 h
of one-to-one lessons on italki
2
English Munchers conversation meetups
10
interviews, 8 students and 2 teachers
1
field study, following one learner in his classroom
"When I'm practicing a conversation in class, it never feels real. I never leave the room." Ernani, the learner I followed in class. Today he's my cofounder at Gecko.

Two things came up again and again. Practice never felt like a real situation, and people were afraid of being judged while they got it wrong. So the brief became a place to rehearse everyday conversations privately, with feedback on whether you were understood.

Four prototype cycles, each testing one question

Cycle 1

Can a 360° video make you feel you're in the room?

Learned: the camera sat too close to the actor, and my fix for its height left a smudge in the footage that broke the immersion. Viewers still asked to see more.

Cycle 2

How do words live inside a scene?

Learned: VR can't hold long text. I resized the panels many times, and dialogues had to get shorter to stay readable.

Cycle 3

Does the whole flow work, filmed in a real bar?

You pick a level and a place, order a drink by speaking, and finish with a score and one thing to improve.

Learned: planning the flow frame by frame worked. The footage didn't: low light and bar noise ruined it. Next time the dialogue gets its own audio channel.

Cycle 4

Can a machine decide whether you were understood?

With a developer, I tested a speech path through Cortana, Microsoft LUIS and a question-and-answer knowledge base, which judges whether an answer counts before the scene moves on. It held up in a simple test, and I ran more headset sessions with users.

What I took from it

Two real learners tested the prototype. Users asked for more scenes after every cycle. The weak points were footage quality and legibility on a phone held 5 cm from the eyes, and the thesis ends by saying it still needed a real build and a business model. The idea I kept: the scene doesn't move on until the answer has been checked.
